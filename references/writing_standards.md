# Writing standards

The reader is an operations manager with five minutes before the day starts. Every line should help them decide or act.

## Structure
- Lead with the conclusion, then the evidence. The first paragraph of the report says what needs doing today.
- One idea per sentence. One action per line.
- Tables for anything compared across jobs. Prose only for patterns and decisions.

## Wording
- Plain UK English: "organise", "colour", "programme of works".
- State facts directly: "Panels invoiced at £112.32 against an agreed £104.00 (+8.0%)."
- Where you aren't sure, say what would settle it: "Confirm against the DNO approval letter." Don't hedge with
  "seems", "appears" or "may possibly".
- Actions start with a verb and name the reference: "Request a credit note for £332.80 against BFW-24115."
- Name owners by role or company: "Installer Team B", "Office (compliance)", "Brightfield Wholesale Ltd". Never
  name customers; use the customer reference.
- Use the industry's terms correctly: G98/G99 notification, DNO, MCS certificate, EIC, commissioning, handover
  pack, kWp.

## Numbers and dates
- Money: `£1,450.00`. Percentages to one decimal place: `+8.0%`.
- Dates: `24 Nov 2026`. Durations in days: `60 days after commissioning (allowed 28)`.
- Quote both sides of every mismatch: `tracker 12 panels / installer 10 panels`.

## Avoid
- Filler and throat-clearing ("It is important to note", "worth noting", "In summary").
- Dramatic framing ("not X, but Y"), exclamation marks, rhetorical questions.
- Em dashes. Use a full stop, colon or comma instead.
- Calling anything "resolved", "fixed" or "complete" unless the data shows it.
- Inventing causes, dates or amounts that aren't in the evidence.
- Mentioning how the report was produced (file names, "the checker", "flagged by the script", rule numbers) in the
  body. The reader needs the business reason, e.g. "a possible connection without DNO approval", not the mechanism.
  The footnote already explains the method.
